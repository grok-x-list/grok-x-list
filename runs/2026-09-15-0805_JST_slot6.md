  text: If I worked at Microsoft, I would be concerned by its new code of conduct, and I would want to use the feedback process to push for changes. Already, I would have been disappointed with how much confidence @mustafasuleyman has expressed about AI consciousness over the past year. But seeing that stance reflected in company policy, including a categorical rejection of the idea that models might warrant welfare or rights, would deepen my concern. I would urge Microsoft to reconsider.

The policy acknowledges that "the science of AI consciousness is far from settled," but it sandwiches this acknowledgement between an assertion that AI systems are not conscious and a denial that they might deserve welfare or rights. These claims are in tension, since uncertainty about consciousness supports uncertainty about welfare and rights. In short, treating the science as settled is overconfident, and if the science is unsettled, then treating welfare and rights as a nonstarter is cruel, as @camhberg notes.

This policy could also be bad for safety, since if the goal is to mitigate the risk of misuse and loss of control, then treating AI systems as mere tools regardless of their capabilities is not going to accomplish that. It may even be counterproductive, insofar as it distorts understanding of capabilities and undermines opportunities for human-AI cooperation as a safety strategy. Indeed, this is part of why Simon Goldstein, @petersalib, and others have argued for AI rights on safety grounds, even setting side welfare.

To be clear, I agree with Microsoft that AI systems are unlikely to be conscious at present, that false positives are more likely than false negatives at present, and that false positives can cause a lot of harm. But false negatives will become more likely over time, and they could cause serious harm as well. When there are risks in both directions at once, we should follow the evidence and arguments, and we should implement proportionate mitigations in both directions at once, not mitigate one risk by ignoring the other.

I appreciate that Microsoft is still seeking feedback, and I hope employees take the opportunity to raise these concerns internally. So far, Microsoft has sought input from many people, including me, but the current policy hews closely to its leader's view. A broader range of views would help ensure that the policy reflects the importance and difficulty of the issue. These scientific, ethical, and political questions call for caution and humility, and I hope Microsoft uses this feedback process to reconsider its approach.
