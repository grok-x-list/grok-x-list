text: I understand. And I'm really trying not to get dragged into a discussion on journals etc. which much more eloquent people than I have been doing for years now. I will just say 
1) I think the reason for these scandals, and in general for many problems, is the reliance on journal names, publishers, etc. by decision-makers as proxies for quality assessment, instead of evaluating the actual value of the science. Obviously that's difficult to do given the volume of applicants etc., I'm not saying I have a brilliant alternative, but I think it's where the misaligned incentive problem starts - offloading a hard judgement decision onto journal names or publishers in the first place.
2) I reject the idea (I'm not saying you mean this, just my opinion in general) that the main problem with all of this is some set of predatory publishers. The whole publication system itself is highly problematic, which many people have pointed out before.
3) clearly, some journals/publishers are worse than others, but even a paper in Nature or similar just means that: an editor decided the story was aligned with the journal's intent (i.e., will keep up the prestige of the platform), ~4 people in the community (referees) liked it, and the author paid thousands of $ to have it printed (and others will have to pay more to read it).
4) we have free-to-read preprints - this is why I preprint everything, as do many others. That way readers can focus on the science content and not worry about journals or publishers.
5) I suggest to readers that no journal guarantees the ideas are good - don't rely on the journal name, or on the small set of reviewers who passed it - plenty of stories of top journals who rejected Nobel-winning work, or had to retract stuff they did publish. If you can evaluate the ideas (especially in conceptual papers where you don't need to replicate experiments), do that yourself based on the content. If you can't, you can always suspend judgement and wait until it either leads to future advances or it doesn't.
