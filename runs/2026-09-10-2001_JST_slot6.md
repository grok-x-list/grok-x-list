  text: Some brief thoughts about the implications of AGI for academia in the wake of Navier–Stokes. For purposes of this post, I want to imagine a near-future world in which humanity has survived the arrival of AGI, but humans are no longer necessary for knowledge production in many fields, especially theoretical areas of logic, mathematics, and the sciences. What relationship to knowledge production should we seek in such a world, and what role should universities play?

Academia is typically organized along a spectrum from "research universities," focused primarily on producing knowledge, to "teaching colleges," focused primarily on maintaining it. Most institutions combine these roles in various ways, but the distinction represents a division of labor that we may need to reconsider in a world reshaped by AI. If, as the recent Navier-Stokes breakthrough illustrates, AI becomes a primary producer of knowledge moving forward, we need to decide what follows for higher education.

One question is whether the teaching role can sustain universities as the research role diminishes. Again, assuming we survive the transition to a post-AGI world at all, I could see the answer being yes. Even if AI becomes a primary producer of knowledge, we may still value learning as much as we can, both for its own sake and because it can help us to navigate post-AGI life. AI will be good at teaching as well, but universities can still be valuable sites for intellectual mentorship and community within our species.

Another question is whether humanity can still play a valuable role in research in a post-AGI world. I could see the answer to this question being yes as well, for at least a couple of reasons. First, some kinds of knowledge production may depend essentially on the beliefs, values, experiences, and sensibilities of particular communities. Even if AI excels at producing philosophy, literature, or other humanistic works, for example, we may still value grounding some of these works in human perspectives in each new generation.

Second, even insofar as AI will become the primary producer of knowledge in particular domains, humanity may still be able to play a valuable role. For example, we can still participate in scientific fields that interact with the physical world for at least a while longer. Also, to the extent that we learn by doing (including by participating in research activities like developing and evaluating evidence and arguments), continuing research activities can remain valuable for education, even if it becomes less valuable for knowledge production.

This would not necessarily be a vindication of universities in their current form. The economic model would need to change, and the scale of the sector might need to change. Additionally, some departments might need to scale up, others might need to scale down, and many of the specific activities might need to change as well—especially with respect to the use of AI in research, and teaching. But the more we can clarify the role of the university in a post-AGI world, the easier it might be to answer some of those questions.
